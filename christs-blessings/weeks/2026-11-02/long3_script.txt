## Coming home empty | parchment
Two women walk along a dusty road toward a small town called Bethlehem. One is older, worn down by years of loss. The other is young, and a foreigner, and she has just given up everything she knew to stay beside her.
When they arrive, the whole town comes out to look. And the older woman says, "Don't call me by my name anymore. Call me Bitter."
This is the story of Ruth and Naomi. It's only four short chapters long, but it holds some of the most tender moments in the whole Bible: grief that is spoken out loud, a love that refuses to leave, and a God who works quietly through ordinary kindness.
If you've lost someone, or if life has left you feeling emptier than you used to be, this story is for you. Let's walk through it together.
## How it all began | hills_sunset
The book opens like this: "Now it came to pass in the days when the judges ruled, that there was a famine in the land. And a certain man of Bethlehemjudah went to sojourn in the country of Moab, he, and his wife, and his two sons."
The man's name was Elimelech. His wife was Naomi. Their sons were Mahlon and Chilion. Moab lay across the Dead Sea, a few days' walk away, and there was food there.
So they went, hoping it would be for a short while. But the years passed. Then the Bible says simply: "And Elimelech Naomi's husband died; and she was left, and her two sons."
The sons married two young women from Moab, named Orpah and Ruth. The family stayed about ten years. Then the hardest line of all: "And Mahlon and Chilion died also both of them; and the woman was left of her two sons and her husband."
In those days a widow with no sons often had no income, no protection and no one to speak for her. Naomi had lost her family, and with them, her future.
## The road back | hills_sunset
Then news came from home. Naomi "had heard in the country of Moab how that the LORD had visited his people in giving them bread." The famine was over. So she set out for Bethlehem, and her two daughters-in-law went with her.
Partway along the road, Naomi stopped. She loved these young women, and she wanted something better for them than her own empty life. She said, "Go, return each to her mother's house: the LORD deal kindly with you, as ye have dealt with the dead, and with me."
They wept together. Orpah kissed her mother-in-law goodbye and went home, and the Bible doesn't blame her for it. It was a sensible choice.
But "Ruth clave unto her." She held on.
## Whither thou goest | calm_dawn
And then Ruth spoke the words that people still read at weddings and at funerals, three thousand years later:
"Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God: Where thou diest, will I die, and there will I be buried: the LORD do so to me, and more also, if ought but death part thee and me."
Think of what this cost her. Ruth was leaving her own family, her language, her land, and any real hope of remarrying in a place where she'd always be a stranger. She had nothing to gain. She simply would not let Naomi walk that road alone.
## Lesson 1: Some love is shown by staying | calm_dawn
Ruth didn't have a solution for Naomi's grief. She couldn't bring anyone back. She couldn't fix the future.
What she could do was stay.
Sometimes we hold back from people who are grieving, because we're afraid of saying the wrong thing. But often the most loving thing isn't a speech. It's turning up, sitting down, and not leaving. If you're the one who is hurting today, perhaps there's a Ruth in your life you haven't noticed. And if someone near you is hurting, perhaps you're being asked to be that Ruth for them.
## "Call me Mara" | parchment
So the two women came to Bethlehem, "and it came to pass, when they were come to Bethlehem, that all the city was moved about them, and they said, Is this Naomi?"
Her name meant pleasant. And she answered: "Call me not Naomi, call me Mara: for the Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full and the LORD hath brought me home again empty: why then call ye me Naomi, seeing the LORD hath testified against me, and the Almighty hath afflicted me?"
These are raw words. Naomi says them to her neighbours, and she says them about God.
## Lesson 2: You are allowed to tell God how it feels | parchment
What's striking is that the Bible keeps her words. It doesn't edit them out or correct her on the spot. Naomi is honest about her pain, and God stays in the story with her.
Notice too: she said she came home empty. But she didn't. Ruth was standing right beside her. Grief can do that. It can make it hard to see the love that is still with us.
If you're carrying grief today, you don't have to pretend in your prayers. You can tell God exactly how it feels. Honest prayer is still prayer. And if the weight feels like too much, please reach out to someone you trust, or to a local helpline. You don't have to carry it alone.
## Gleaning in the barley field | sunrise_lake
They arrived "in the beginning of barley harvest." And God's law already had a kindness built into it. Farmers were told not to harvest the very edges of their fields, and not to go back for what they dropped. The leftovers were for the poor, the foreigner and the widow. That was called gleaning.
Ruth was all three. So she said to Naomi, "Let me now go to the field, and glean ears of corn after him in whose sight I shall find grace."
The Bible says, "her hap was to light on a part of the field belonging unto Boaz, who was of the kindred of Elimelech." It reads like a coincidence. But the whole story is quietly asking: was it?
Boaz noticed the young woman working hard in the heat. Ruth bowed and asked him, "Why have I found grace in thine eyes, that thou shouldest take knowledge of me, seeing I am a stranger?"
He had heard about her already. He said, "It hath fully been shewed me, all that thou hast done unto thy mother in law since the death of thine husband." And then he blessed her: "The LORD recompense thy work, and a full reward be given thee of the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings thou art come to trust."
Then, out of her hearing, Boaz told his workers: "let fall also some of the handfuls of purpose for her, and leave them, that she may glean them, and rebuke her not."
## Lesson 3: God's kindness often wears ordinary clothes | sunrise_lake
There are no angels in the book of Ruth. No fire from heaven. No voice from the clouds.
Instead, God's care comes through a fair law, a hard-working young woman, and a farmer who quietly drops extra barley on purpose so she won't feel like a charity case.
That evening, when Ruth came home with her arms full, something shifted in Naomi. She said, "Blessed be he of the LORD, who hath not left off his kindness to the living and to the dead."
Bitter Naomi had just spoken a blessing. The first small light was coming back.
Sometimes we wait for God to answer in a dramatic way, and we miss the handfuls He's leaving along our path: a meal from a neighbour, a friend's text, an unexpected bit of help. Those are worth noticing, and worth thanking Him for.
## From empty to full | hills_sunset
In the chapters that follow, Naomi starts to hope again and helps Ruth make a brave request. Boaz was one of the family's close relatives, what the law called a kinsman, someone with the right to redeem a family's land and care for its widows. Boaz goes to the town gate, settles the matter honourably in front of the elders, and marries Ruth.
Then the story comes full circle. "So Boaz took Ruth, and she was his wife." In time, the Bible says, the LORD gave her a son.
The same neighbours who heard Naomi say "call me Mara" now gather around her and say: "Blessed be the LORD, which hath not left thee this day without a kinsman, that his name may be famous in Israel. And he shall be unto thee a restorer of thy life, and a nourisher of thine old age: for thy daughter in law, which loveth thee, which is better to thee than seven sons, hath born him."
"And Naomi took the child, and laid it in her bosom, and became nurse unto it."
The woman who said she came home empty is now holding new life in her arms.
## Lesson 4: Empty is not the end of your story | calm_dawn
The book closes with a name. The baby was called Obed, and the Bible says, "he is the father of Jesse, the father of David." Ruth, the foreigner who chose to stay, became the great-grandmother of King David. And centuries later, Matthew's Gospel names her in the family line of Jesus.
Naomi never saw most of that. She only saw the next step: a field, a meal, a kind man, a baby in her lap. But God was weaving something far bigger than she could imagine.
Your loss is real, and this story doesn't ask you to pretend otherwise. Naomi's husband and sons did not come back. But it does say that empty is not where God leaves us. He keeps working, often slowly, often through other people, in ways we may only understand much later.
## A prayer for the empty-handed | calm_dawn
Let's pray.
Lord, some of us feel like Naomi today. We went out full, and we've come home empty. Thank You that we can tell You honestly how it feels. Open our eyes to the Ruths You have placed beside us, and to the handfuls of kindness You leave along our path. Where we feel bitter, bring back blessing. Where we feel empty, fill us again in Your time. And help us be faithful friends to others who are grieving. In Jesus' name, Amen.
